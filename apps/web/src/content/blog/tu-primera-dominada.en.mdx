The pull-up is calisthenics' entrance exam. It is the movement that separates "I do some exercise at home" from "I train seriously with my own bodyweight" — and it is also the one most people quit before earning.

The reason is almost never genetics or bodyweight. It is that most people try to earn pull-ups by **doing pull-ups**: they hang off the bar, pull with everything they have, move nowhere, and repeat that three times a week for months. It is like trying to learn to run a marathon by running marathons.

A pull-up is built in pieces. This guide is the ladder that works.

## Why it is not happening yet

When someone hangs and cannot move an inch, one of three things is failing — and none of them is "arm strength":

- **You cannot engage your back.** A pull-up is driven by the lats, not the biceps. If you start the movement by bending your elbows, you have turned it into a biceps curl with your entire bodyweight on the bar. You will never win that curl.
- **You cannot hang long enough.** If your grip gives out at 15 seconds, your forearms quit before your back has done any work. Grip is the invisible bottleneck.
- **You have never performed the full path.** Your nervous system does not own the pattern. Even with enough strength, it cannot yet coordinate scapula, elbow and trunk in the right order.

<Callout variant="tip" title="The 80% rule">
  If you cannot hang from the bar with straight arms for **30 seconds**, that is your first goal — not any of the ones below it. No grip, no pull-up.
</Callout>

## The five-rung ladder

Every rung has a measurable criterion. Do not move up because three weeks have passed: move up when you hit the number. And if you stall two weeks in a row, drop back a rung and consolidate.

<Ladder>

<ProgressionStep n={1} title="Hang and hold" goal="30 unbroken seconds">
  <ExerciseLink id="dead_hang">Dead Hang</ExerciseLink> — shoulder-width grip, arms straight, shoulders relaxed. Start wherever you land, even if that is 8 seconds. Accumulate **2 to 3 total minutes** per session across as many sets as it takes.

  If your hands hurt more than your arms, that is the right kind of hurt: it is skin and grip adapting, and it settles within a couple of weeks.
</ProgressionStep>

<ProgressionStep n={2} title="Engage the scapula" goal="3 sets of 8 clean reps">
  This is the rung most people skip and later pay for. Hanging with **completely straight arms**, depress your shoulders to lift your body an inch or two without bending your elbows at any point.

  The range of motion is absurdly small, and that is the point: it isolates the first segment of the pull-up, the one almost nobody performs correctly. Start with the <ExerciseLink id="active_hang_scapular">Scapular Active Hang</ExerciseLink> and graduate to <ExerciseLink id="scapular_pull_up">scapular pull-ups</ExerciseLink> once you own it.
</ProgressionStep>

<ProgressionStep n={3} title="Horizontal rows" goal="3 sets of 10 with the body near horizontal">
  The <ExerciseLink id="australian_pull_up">Australian Pull-up</ExerciseLink> is a pull-up lying down: same pulling pattern, but you choose how much weight you lift by changing your body angle. Use a low bar, rings, or even a sturdy table.

  The closer to horizontal you get, the heavier it is. Once 10 horizontal reps feel comfortable, put your feet on a bench with the <ExerciseLink id="feet_elevated_australian_pull_up">feet-elevated Australian</ExerciseLink>.
</ProgressionStep>

<ProgressionStep n={4} title="Negatives" goal="5 reps with a 5-second descent">
  This rung builds the most strength, and it is the one most people botch.

  Use a chair or a jump to get **your chin above the bar**, then lower as slowly as you possibly can. Your first attempt will probably be a 2-second drop with no control. The target is 5 seconds.

  You are roughly 30 to 40% stronger lowering than lifting: <ExerciseLink id="neg_pullup">negative pull-ups</ExerciseLink> exploit exactly that margin.
</ProgressionStep>

<ProgressionStep n={5} title="Band-assisted" goal="3 sets of 5 with your thinnest band">
  The <ExerciseLink id="assisted_pull_up_band">band-assisted pull-up</ExerciseLink> is the final bridge. Put a foot or knee in the band and perform the full range of motion.

  Strip the assistance away in stages: thick band, medium band, thin band, nothing. Do not jump from "5 reps with a thin band" straight to "attempt one unassisted" — that is the gap where people get frustrated and quit.
</ProgressionStep>

</Ladder>

Once you clear rung five, attempt an unassisted <ExerciseLink id="pullup_strict">strict pull-up</ExerciseLink>. No swinging, no kipping, no leg kick: chin over the bar and a controlled descent.

## How to schedule it

Three sessions a week, with at least one rest day between them. Elbow tendons adapt more slowly than muscle, and training pulling every day is the fast lane to tendinitis.

| Day | Primary rung | Volume |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monday | Your current rung | 4 sets |
| Wednesday | One rung below | 3 sets, higher reps |
| Friday | Your current rung | 4 sets |

Rest **2 to 3 minutes** between sets. This is strength training, not conditioning: if you finish a set out of breath, you rested too little. Always leave one or two reps in reserve — going to failure on every set multiplies fatigue without speeding up progress.

<Callout variant="warn" title="The most common mistake">
  Switching progressions every week because "this one is not working". Tendons take **6 to 8 weeks** to adapt. Stay on a rung until you hit its criterion, even when it gets boring.
</Callout>

## How long it takes

With three weekly sessions and genuine consistency:

- If you already do push-ups and can hang, **6 to 10 weeks**.
- Starting from absolute zero, **4 to 6 months**.
- If you are carrying significant extra weight, a calorie deficit will speed this up more than any progression: every kilo you drop is a kilo you do not have to pull.

That range is deliberately wide. What does not change is the order of the ladder.

<KeyTakeaways title="Key takeaways">
  - **Grip comes first.** 30 seconds hanging before anything else.
  - **The back starts the movement**, not the arms. That is why rung 2 is not optional.
  - **Negatives are the engine.** That is where the strength is actually built.
  - **Criteria, not calendars.** Move up when you hit the number, not when the weeks pass.
  - **Three days a week.** More is not better: the tendons set the pace.
</KeyTakeaways>
